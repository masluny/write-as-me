Should every student study science until the end of secondary school?

Science education gives young people more than a list of facts. It teaches them how to ask questions, test ideas and weigh evidence — skills that matter far beyond the laboratory. For this reason, I believe science should remain a core part of the curriculum for all students.

First, scientific literacy is essential for informed citizenship. Decisions about public health, energy and climate policy increasingly depend on an understanding of basic scientific concepts. Students who stop studying science early may find it harder to evaluate the claims they encounter in the news and on social media.

Second, science develops transferable skills. Designing an experiment requires careful planning, while interpreting results encourages critical thinking and intellectual honesty. These habits are valuable in any career, from business to the arts.

That said, it is important to acknowledge the concerns of students who struggle with these subjects. Not every learner enjoys chemistry or physics, and a rigid curriculum can undermine motivation. Schools could address this by offering applied courses that connect scientific principles to everyday life, such as nutrition, technology or environmental studies.

In conclusion, while the delivery of science education can and should be more flexible, the subject itself remains a vital foundation. Ensuring that all students continue to engage with science will prepare them to navigate an increasingly complex world.
